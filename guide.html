Recruiter and Behavioral Round

This round focuses on your background, communication skills, and fit for the role.

Introductory Questions

  • Tell me about yourself.
  • Why do you want this role or company?
  • What are your core strengths?
  • What is your biggest weakness?
  • Why are you leaving your current job?
  • Where do you see yourself in 5 years?
  • What are your salary expectations?

Situational and STAR Method Questions

  • Describe a challenging project you have worked on.
  • How do you handle pressure or tight deadlines?
  • Tell me about a time you made a data driven recommendation that was not followed.
  • How do you approach explaining complex analyses to non technical stakeholders?
  • Tell me about a project you are particularly proud of.
  • Walk me through an analysis you did and the impact it had.
  • Describe a time when you had to deal with a difficult stakeholder.

The Core Strategy: STAR Method

For every behavioral question, do not meander. Use the STAR framework to structure your answers concisely.

  • S - Situation: Briefly describe the context or problem. (e.g., "We noticed a 15% drop in user engagement.")
  • T - Task: Explain your specific responsibility. (e.g., "I was tasked with identifying the root cause.")
  • A - Action: Describe what YOU did (not just "we"). Focus on your analysis, the tools used, and the decisions made. (e.g., "I performed a cohort analysis in SQL...")
  • R - Result: Quantify the outcome. (e.g., "This led to a 10% reduction in churn.")

Recruiter Round Tips (The "Fit" Check)

While the principles above are crucial for the behavioral rounds, the Recruiter Round (Day 1) is about making a strong first impression.

  • "Tell me about yourself": Keep it to 2 minutes. Highlight your current role, relevant past experience, and why you want to join this specific company (mentioning their principles is a great touch).
  • "Why Amazon?": Connect your answer to their specific products (e.g., AWS, Prime) or culture (Leadership Principles). Avoid generic answers; show you've done your research.
  • "What are your strengths?": Pick 2-3 strengths relevant to data analysis (e.g., "Turning complex data into actionable insights") and back them up with examples.
  • "What is your biggest weakness?": Be honest but choose a weakness that isn't critical to the role. Show self-awareness and the steps you are taking to improve.

The 16 Leadership Principles & Sample Questions

Recruiters and hiring managers will ask questions designed to see if you embody these values. Prepare 2 stories for each principle using the STAR method.

  • I. Customer Obsession: Leaders start with the customer and work backward. They work vigorously to earn and keep customer trust.
    • Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult customer.
    • Describe a time you didn't meet customer expectations. How did you fix it?
    • How do you prioritize customer needs when you have conflicting data?
  • II. Ownership: Leaders are owners. They think long-term and don't sacrifice long-term value for short-term results. They never say "that's not my job."
    • Tell me about a time you took on a task outside your job description.
    • Describe a time you had to work on a project with unclear responsibilities.
  • III. Invent and Simplify: Leaders expect and require innovation and invention from their teams and always find ways to simplify.
    • Describe a time you found a simple solution to a complex data problem.
    • Tell me about a time you automated a manual process to save time.
    • 2026 note: A strong answer increasingly mentions automating a manual step using a script or an AI assistant, not just an Excel macro — this signals you simplify with current tools.
  • IV. Are Right, A Lot: Leaders have strong judgment and good instincts. They seek diverse perspectives and work to disconfirm their beliefs.
    • Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete data.
    • Describe a time you were wrong. How did you handle it?
  • V. Learn and Be Curious: Leaders are never done learning and always seek to improve themselves.
    • Tell me about an important lesson you learned in the past year.
    • Describe a situation that changed your perspective on a technical approach.
  • VI. Hire and Develop the Best: Leaders raise the performance bar with every hire and promotion. They recognize talent and move them throughout the organization.
    • Tell me about a time you mentored a junior analyst.
    • Describe a time you helped a colleague improve their performance.
  • VII. Insist on the Highest Standards: Leaders have relentlessly high standards. They ensure that defects do not get sent down the line and that problems are fixed.
    • Tell me about a time you were dissatisfied with the quality of a project. What did you do?
    • Describe a time you motivated a team to go above and beyond.
  • VIII. Think Big: Leaders create and communicate a bold direction that inspires results. They think differently and look around corners for ways to serve customers.
    • Tell me about your most significant professional achievement.
    • Describe a time you proposed a bold new strategy based on your analysis.
  • IX. Bias for Action: Speed matters in business. Many decisions and actions are reversible and do not need extensive study. Calculated risk-taking is valued.
    • Describe a time you took a calculated risk to meet a deadline.
    • Tell me about a time you acted to fix a problem without waiting for permission.
  • X. Frugality: Accomplish more with less. Constraints breed resourcefulness, self-sufficiency, and invention.
    • Tell me about a time you had to complete a project with limited resources or budget.
    • Describe a time you had to be resourceful to get the data you needed.
  • XI. Earn Trust: Leaders listen attentively, speak candidly, and treat others respectfully. They are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing.
    • Describe a time you had to speak up in a difficult environment.
    • Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news (e.g., poor metrics) to a stakeholder.
  • XII. Dive Deep: Leaders operate at all levels, stay connected to the details, audit frequently, and are skeptical when metrics and anecdote differ.
    • Tell me about the most complex problem you solved using deep data analysis.
    • Give an example of a time you used data to find the root cause of a specific issue.
  • XIII. Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit: Leaders are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree, even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting. Once a decision is determined, they commit wholly.
    • Describe a time you disagreed with a team member or manager's approach.
    • Tell me about an unpopular decision you made and how you handled it.
  • XIV. Deliver Results: Leaders focus on the key inputs for their business and deliver them with the right quality and in a timely fashion.
    • Describe a situation where you faced multiple conflicting deadlines. How did you handle it?
    • Tell me about a time your team was struggling, and you helped them push through to success.
  • XV. Strive to be Earth's Best Employer: Leaders work every day to create a safer, more productive, higher-performing, more diverse, and more just work environment.
    • Give an example of a time you fostered an inclusive environment in your team.
    • How do you handle varying strengths and weaknesses within a project team?
  • XVI. Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility: We must be humble and thoughtful about even the secondary effects of our actions. Leaders create more than they consume and always leave things better than how they found them.
    • Describe a moral or ethical dilemma you faced at work.
    • Tell me about a time you left a project or process in a better state than you found it.

SQL

Database Fundamentals

Before diving into SQL queries, it is essential to understand the foundational concepts of databases:

  • What is a Database (DB)? A database is an organized collection of data stored and accessed electronically. It allows efficient data retrieval and management.
  • What is SQL? SQL (Structured Query Language) is a programming language used to interact with databases. It helps perform operations like retrieving, updating, or managing data.
  • What is a Query? A query is a set of instructions written in SQL to communicate with a database and perform specific tasks, such as retrieving data.
  • What is a Database Management System (DBMS)? DBMS is software that provides an interface to manage databases, allowing users to store, retrieve, and manipulate data.
  • What is a Relational Database Management System (RDBMS)? RDBMS is a type of DBMS that organizes data into tables with rows and columns, ensuring data relationships are maintained.
  • What is a SQL Client? A SQL client is a tool or software used to connect to and interact with databases using SQL.
  • What is Normalization? Normalization is the process of organizing data in a database to reduce redundancy and improve data integrity.
  • Key Database Connection Information: To connect to a database, you need: Hostname (The address of the database server), Username (Your login ID for the database), Database name (The specific database to connect to), Password (The authentication key), and Port (The communication endpoint).

SQL Fundamentals & Intermediate Concepts

  • Basic SQL Commands: SELECT Statement (Used to retrieve data from a database), WHERE Clause (Filters records based on conditions), and CRUD Operations (Create, Read, Update, Delete).
  • ORDER BY Clause: Allows sorting of data in ascending or descending order.
  • Joins: Combine data from multiple tables based on a related column (Inner Join, Left Join, Right Join, Outer Join, Self Join).
  • Aggregation Functions: SUM (Adds up numeric data), COUNT (Counts the number of rows), AVG (Calculates the average), GROUP BY (Groups rows sharing a property and performs aggregate functions).
  • Subqueries: Nested queries used within another SQL query.
  • Window Functions: Perform calculations across a set of table rows related to the current row.
  • Common Table Expressions (CTEs): Temporary result sets defined within a SQL query for better readability and reusability.
  • Indexes and Optimization: Improve query performance by indexing frequently accessed columns.

Optimization & Order of Operations

SQL does NOT execute queries in the same order it is written in.

  • SQL commands are written in this order: SELECT, FROM, JOIN, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, ORDER BY, LIMIT.
  • But are executed in this order: FROM, JOIN, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, SELECT, ORDER BY, LIMIT.
  • How do we use SQL order of operations to write queries?
    • Start by optimizing the FROM clause: When using multiple tables, always start with the smallest table to reduce initial data volume.
    • Optimize your JOINs using the SQL order of operations. JOINs are one of the first to be executed & they are computationally expensive.
    • Order your JOINs to start with the smallest dataset that gets increasingly larger.
    • Ensure joined columns are indexed for faster matching.
    • Use ON clauses to reduce the joined dataset size early in the process.
    • Optimize the WHERE clause since filtering happens early in the execution process.
    • Use WHERE instead of HAVING (if possible): WHERE gets executed before HAVING, so put filters in the WHERE clause for better efficiency.
    • Avoid filtering on HAVING (if possible): Reserve HAVING for filtering only aggregated data, as it's processed after GROUP BY.
    • BE SELECTIVE IN YOUR SELECT: Only SELECT the columns you need, as this can impact performance, Especially when dealing with very wide datasets.
    • Remove any unnecessary ORDER BY clauses.

Techniques for Effective Data Cleaning

  • Replace NULL values with a default value or remove them based on the context: SELECT COALESCE(column_name, 'DefaultValue') FROM table_name;
  • Identify and delete duplicate rows based on key columns using a CTE and ROW_NUMBER().
  • Convert text to lower or upper case to ensure consistency: SELECT LOWER(column_name) AS cleaned_column FROM table_name;
  • Remove extra spaces from text fields: SELECT TRIM(column_name) FROM table_name;
  • Convert date strings into a consistent date format: SELECT STR_TO_DATE(column_name, ‘%m/%d/%Y') FROM table_name;
  • Identify and manage outliers in numerical data using BETWEEN lower_limit AND upper_limit.
  • Replace or remove special characters in text fields: SELECT REGEXP_REPLACE(column_name, '[^a-zA-Z0-9] ', '') AS cleaned_column FROM table_name;
  • Standardize values in categorical columns: UPDATE table_name SET column_name = 'Male' WHERE column_name IN ('M', 'male') ;
  • Use statistical methods to fill in missing numerical data: UPDATE table_name SET column_name = (SELECT AVG(column_name) FROM table_name) WHERE column_name IS NULL;

Product Sense and Case Studies

These questions test your ability to think critically about business problems and metrics.

  • Measuring Success:
    • YouTube introduces a 10 second video format. What metrics would you use to measure its success?
    • YouTube launches a live streaming feature. How would you measure its success?
    • Netflix releases a "Shuffle Play" button. How can you tell if it is driving engagement?
    • Airbnb introduces a loyalty program. How would you evaluate whether it is working?
    • This focuses on how to define the health or success of a new product or feature.
    • Clarify Goals: Ask what the product does, who it is for, and what its primary goal is before suggesting metrics.
    • The "Rule of Three": Aim to provide no more than three metrics: Two Success Metrics (To indicate effectiveness), One Guardrail Metric (A metric that should not degrade).
  • Product Ideation:
    • How would you improve user retention on Reddit?
    • How would you improve Google Calendar?
    • What new feature would you add to Duolingo to increase user motivation?
    • How can Spotify make music discovery more social?
  • Investigation (Metric Drops) & Diagnosing a Problem:
    • TikTok's user engagement dropped by 15 percent. How would you investigate this drop?
    • The number of active listings on eBay decreased by 8%. How do you diagnose?
    • Amazon's average cart abandonment rate has increased by 5%. How would you investigate?
    • The engagement rate on YouTube ads has decreased by 10%. What steps would you take?
    • We noticed a 15% drop in bookings on Booking.com. How would you investigate?
    • This category involves investigating why a metric (e.g., a 20% drop in Facebook group creation) has shifted negatively.
    • Clarify: Define the scenario and the metric precisely (e.g., how is "ETA" calculated?).
    • Time Aspect: Did the change happen suddenly or progressively?
    • Internal vs. External Factors: Check for bugs/data issues (internal) vs. seasonality, competitors, or special events (external).
    • Segmentation: Break down the data by region, platform (iOS/Android), or user demographics to isolate the cause.
    • Decompose the Metric: Break the metric into its components (e.g., $DAU = Existing + New + Resurrected - Churned$) to find the specific driver.
  • Launch or Not:
    • This involves deciding whether to release a feature based on experimental results.
    • Experiment Design: Explain how to split users into control/treatment groups and determine the duration of the test.
    • Conflicting Results: If one metric goes up but another goes down, translate the trade-off into business impact.
    • Long-term vs. Short-term: Consider if a short-term drawback (like a minor bounce rate increase) is worth the long-term benefit (user growth).

ML Tips

General ML Tips

  • Understand the business problem, then frame it as an ML problem: Otherwise you will build a great ML model, that does not move the business metric it was intended to move.
  • What is the business metric we want to improve? It is crucial you talk with all relevant stakeholders at the beginning of the project. They have more business context than you and can help you understand what is the target you need to shoot at.
  • What is the baseline we need to beat? Is there any solution currently working to solve this, like some rule-based heuristics? If there is one, this is the benchmark you have to beat in order to have a business impact. If there is none, you can have a quick win by implementing a non-ML solution.
  • Will the model be used as a black box or as a tool to assist humans to make better decisions? Creating black-box solutions is easier than explainable ones. If you work in healthcare, for example, you need explain ability. If you work in algorithmic trading, you don’t.
  • 2026 note: With LLM-based products, this question now extends to explainability of generative outputs too — e.g. citing sources, showing retrieved context (RAG), or producing a confidence score, rather than just explaining a classifier's weights. [2026 UPDATE]
  • Focus on getting more and better data: In Kaggle competitions everyone gets the same data and competes purely on modeling. In reality, the opposite happens — the data, not the model, is usually the bottleneck.
  • Talk (a lot) with data engineering guys. They know where each bit of data lives, can help you fetch it, and can build pipelines for useful 3rd-party data.
  • Be fluent in SQL. SQL is the most universal way to access data — especially in less data-mature environments like startups. It lets you build training data quickly.
  • Structure your code well: Jupyter notebooks are great for quick prototyping, but they get crowded and unmanageable fast. Graduate experiments into proper modules/scripts once an idea proves out.
  • 2026 reality check: this matters even more now — interviewers and teams expect candidates to show production-ready repos (clean structure, tests, a README, not a single giant notebook) as evidence of real engineering ability. [2026 UPDATE]

What's Changed Going Into 2026

  • Classical ML + GenAI, not classical ML or GenAI. The market wants engineers who can choose the right tool — sometimes that's a Random Forest, sometimes an LLM with RAG — and explain the cost/latency trade-off either way.
  • Production fluency is the differentiator. Multiple 2026 guides note that candidates increasingly fail not from lacking textbook knowledge, but from being unable to explain a real production failure end-to-end, or what a design choice costs in production.
  • Demand for AI/ML engineers is way up. Industry analysts have flagged AI/ML engineering as one of the most in-demand technical roles for 2026, with a large share of the engineering workforce expected to need GenAI upskilling.
  • AI-augmented hiring is now common. Expect AI-assisted technical assessments, and be ready to demonstrate that you can effectively and critically use AI tools — not just that you can avoid them.
  • Cloud and MLOps tooling fluency is assumed, not a bonus. AWS/Azure/GCP plus at least one of MLflow/Kubeflow/SageMaker Pipelines now shows up as a baseline expectation, not a nice-to-have.

A Few Concrete Things to Add to Your Study Plan

  • Build (or polish) one end-to-end project that includes deployment and monitoring — not just a model in a notebook. This is consistently cited as the single best differentiator.
  • Pick one vector database and one orchestration framework (e.g. pgvector + LangChain, or Pinecone + LlamaIndex) and get hands-on — be able to explain the design choices, not just name-drop them.
  • Practice explaining a real production failure or trade-off you've encountered, end-to-end, in plain language.
  • Don't drop classical ML fundamentals from your prep just because LLM questions are trendy — they're both being tested.
